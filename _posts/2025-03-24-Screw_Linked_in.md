Since I started working at CTW, people have asked me for this surprisingly often. "Why don't you have a LinkedIn profile?" they say, with a tone that suggests I've rejected some fundamental law of professional existence. The same tone as if I were to tell them that I don't have a phone number in 2026. My answer is simple: I don't trust the business model, and I don't want to hand over the keys to my personal data to a company whose entire value proposition to share holders depends on selling it to the highest bidder.

I'm not hiding anything. I don't have two jobs. My CV is open for everyone to see. I'm not living in a cabin in the woods (at least for now). I just don't think that this tradeoff is worth it for me and my values.

## The core problem

LinkedIn, Facebook, Twitter, TikTok, e.t.c; are all part of (and relient on) surveillance capitalism. You fet a free platform to connect with people or post your thoughts, and in return, the company gets to track everything you do, everywhere you go (on and off their platforms if they can), and sell that information to advertisers. The value they're extracting from your data typically dwarfs the value of service you're getting in return.

I understand the appeal. It's convenient. You get recommendations, connections, job leads. The friction of staying in touch is lower. But every time I see a new privacy scandal regarding these comapnies, such as:

- [LinkedIn secretly scanning for chrome extensions](https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/linkedin-secretly-scans-for-6-000-plus-chrome-extensions-collects-data/)
- [Facebook running psychological manipulation experiments on it's users](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facebook_emotional_manipulation_experiment)
- [Twitter selling phone numbers to advertisers](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2023/feb/07/techscape-elon-musk-twitter-api)

I don't want to be part of this.

The thing that gets me is the intentionality of it. The main business model for these companies relies on engineering entire systems to collect the largest amount of data in the most efficient way possible. Infinite scroll, notification designed to trigger habits, algorithms that optimize for engagement over truth. None of this is accidental. They've hired the smartest people in the world to figure out how to make their platforms as addictive as possible while harvesting everything they can learn about you in the process.

## Why LinkedIn specifically

LinkedIn's supposed to be the "professional" social network. And honestly, I can see how so, since the corporate world is already filled with facade and performance. LinkedIn didn't invent the professional theather, the humble-brag, the "I'm thrilled to announce"; it simply gave it a feed and a like a button. 

> Should I even have to mention the amount of r/linkedinlunatics that I saw during my time on linkedin? Should I also mention that the only job enquiries that I received on linkedin were consultancy-slop jobs?

And the parts of LinkedIn that people actually value, i.e. job leads, industry news, e.t.c. do not require a 10 year dossier on my professional life sitting on someone else's servers, monitized in ways I'll never see, and cannot opt out from.

## The tradeoff

I won't pretend that there is no cost to this. There is. It seems that the majority of recruiters default to LinkedIn, so I'll never show up to those who search exclusively there. But I've found that this friction is smaller than advertised. People worth staying in touch with will make the effort to find another way, either email or text message.

The ones who only would have reached out via a LinkedIn connection request were probably never going to turn into anything more than a number in the "connections" count anyway. What I lose in convenience, I get back in not being a product. My employment history, my network graph, my browsing habits are sitting in one less system engineered to extract maximum commercial value from them. That's worth more to me than showing up in one more recruiters' search results.


## The alternative

I don't think opting out of social media has to mean opting out of a professional life. Here is what I am doing to show my work:

- Deploying a personal website with my CV on it (this one!). Anyone who wants to know what I've worked on can see it, no account required, no tracking pixels, no algorithm deciding who gets to see it.
- Using email as the default way to reach me. It works, it's decentralized(-ish), and nobody's mining it for ad targeting.
- Being findable without being harvested. My CV is public. My work is public. If someone wants to hire me or collaborate, the information is there — it's just not wrapped in a platform whose business model depends on knowing more about me than I'd choose to share.

None of this makes me invisible, and it's not a purity test I expect anyone else to pass. If LinkedIn works for you and you've made peace with the tradeoff, that's a reasonable place to land. I just haven't. For me, the convenience isn't worth handing over that much of who I am to a system built to monetize it. I'd rather have a slightly smaller network and keep the terms of the deal on my side.
